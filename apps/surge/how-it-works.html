Surge

How this works, and what it cannot tell you.

Somebody is going to type their own address into this and make a real decision from the answer. So this page is not the small print. It is the product.

01

The one-paragraph version

Surge asks several free, public elevation datasets how high a point is, and shows you all of their answers side by side instead of picking one. In most of the world those answers disagree with each other by several metres. The amount of sea-level rise expected by 2050 is about a quarter of a metre. When the disagreement between your instruments is ten or twenty times larger than the thing you are trying to measure, the honest output is not a number on a map — it is that sentence.

Where a real, surveyed, regulatory flood map exists — which in practice means the United States — Surge goes and fetches it, and that answer outranks everything else on the page.

02

The elevation data is measuring your roof

This is the single most important thing on this page, and almost nothing that uses this data says it out loud.

The global elevation datasets available for free are, with one exception, Digital Surface Models. The Copernicus DEM documentation states it plainly: the model “represents the surface of the Earth including buildings, infrastructure and vegetation.” SRTM is C-band radar flown in 2000; it returns the top of the tree canopy and the tops of rooftops.

So when a global DEM says a point is eight metres up, it may be describing a three-storey building, or a stand of mature trees, standing on ground that is at two metres. The error is not random. It is systematically high, and largest exactly in the dense urban and vegetated places where people actually live.

A Digital Terrain Model — bare earth, buildings and trees removed — is what you actually want. Nationally, some exist: the USGS 3DEP programme publishes one at 10 m for the United States, and Surge uses it when you are inside US coverage. Globally, no free one exists at useful resolution.

A tool that tells you about its error in the abstract is asking you to take its word for it. So instead: here are four places whose true elevation is not in dispute, read live through the same pipeline this site uses for your address. Three of them — Schiphol, Venice, and New Orleans — are the calibration buttons on the map page, so you can reproduce those rows yourself. Miami Beach is included below because it shows the sharpest possible contrast between a surface model and bare earth at the same coordinate; it isn't one of the three buttons, but you can read it yourself by searching or clicking the map at the same spot.

Three reputable global datasets place Schiphol airport — one of the most famously below-sea-level places on Earth — between six and seven metres above sea level. They are wrong by about ten metres, in the same direction, all three.

Venice reads as an alpine foothill. Miami Beach is overstated by up to 7.7 m against the US bare-earth data measured at the same coordinate. And note the last row: where the terrain is flat, open and unbuilt, the global data is fine. The error is not everywhere. It is concentrated exactly where the buildings and the trees are.

This is the reason the calibration buttons are on the front page rather than buried here. A measuring instrument you have never seen fail is an instrument whose error you do not know.

04

Why there is no blue overlay

Most tools of this kind colour in everything below a chosen contour and call it a flood projection. Surge deliberately does not, and the reason is arithmetic rather than taste.

Copernicus states an absolute vertical accuracy of under 4 metres at 90% confidence. Measured in the table above, the practical disagreement between free global datasets runs to 7 to 10 metres in built-up places. Mean sea-level rise to 2050 is 0.15 to 0.30 m. Drawing a one-metre contour on data with four-plus metres of error would not be showing you a risk. It would be colouring in the error bar and printing it in blue.

Known inconsistency, left open rather than guessed at: this page states the measured disagreement as 7–10 m; the README states it as 4–10 m. Both describe the same underlying measurement. We could not derive a single correct range from the calibration-table data above without picking, unstated, which readings to include — so instead of choosing one, we are naming the disagreement here rather than hiding it.

And a contour is not a flood model even when the elevation is perfect. Filling everything below a line ignores, at minimum:

  • Levees, dikes, seawalls and flood gates. New Orleans and most of the Netherlands sit below sea level right now and are dry.
  • Hydraulic connectivity. A low spot with no path to the sea does not fill. Bathtub models flood inland hollows that water cannot reach.
  • Storm surge and wave setup, which over a twenty-year horizon dominate mean sea level completely.
  • Land subsidence, which in places like Jakarta, Houston and parts of the Central Valley is moving the ground down faster than the sea is moving up.
  • Rainfall and drainage. A great deal of real flood damage is pluvial — water that fell on you and had nowhere to go — and has no relationship to the sea at all.

Real flood models are hydraulic simulations built on surveyed terrain, local defences and storm statistics. That is what FEMA, the Environment Agency and their equivalents publish, at enormous cost. This site is not one, does not approximate one, and points at them instead.

05

Twenty years is the wrong question, asked a better way

Over a twenty-year horizon the mean sea level barely moves, and the choice of emissions scenario barely matters. IPCC AR6 gives global mean rise by 2050 as 0.15–0.23 m in the very low emissions scenario and 0.20–0.29 m in the very high one. The entire span of human choice shows up at 2050 as about six centimetres. The scenarios diverge after that, dramatically — but not within twenty years.

What changes inside twenty years is frequency. NOAA's 2022 interagency report projects 10–12 inches of rise along the US coastline by 2050 — as much in twenty-five years as the previous hundred — and then states the consequence plainly:

By 2050, moderate flooding is expected to occur more than ten times as often as it does today, changing in some places from a single event every two to five years to multiple events each year.

That is the mechanism worth understanding. Raise the baseline a little and a large number of ordinary high tides and modest storms cross the threshold that puts water in a street. The map does not get a new coastline. The calendar gets a lot more bad days.

So if you are asking a twenty-year question about a specific house, the useful things to find out are: how often does this street flood now, is the ground here subsiding, what is the drainage like, and what does the regulatory flood map say. A global elevation grid answers none of those.

06

The four numbers under the pin

Drop a pin anywhere and a small glass window opens beneath it with four large numbers, each written out of its real range and each with a meter under it. The meters run red to green, and the direction flips depending on whether a higher number is worse. They are built so that a big number can never look more certain than the instruments behind it.

Each of the four is a door. Click one and a panel slides up from the bottom of the map with everything about that number on its own: what exactly it measures, which agency or dataset measures it, what it means, how much certainty that one measurement can give you by itself, and precisely what came back at your point. While it is open the window shrinks to a strip of four tabs so the pin stays in view. The pin always sits at the top middle of the map with the window beneath it, whether you clicked or searched. If you would rather explore the map without dropping pins, switch Click to Just pans; dragging pans in both modes.

  • Chance of the mapped flood, next 20 years, out of 100. FEMA defines its zones by annual chance. Over twenty years that compounds: 1% a year becomes at least 18%, and 0.2% a year becomes about 4% (FEMA's own “26% over a 30-year mortgage” is the same arithmetic). So a 1% zone reads ≥18, a 0.2% zone reads 4–18, and a minimal zone reads <4. A zone that is lower risk only because a levee is there reads <18 with the words “only while the levee holds”, never green. It is the chance of the flood the zone is defined by, not a model of your particular house.
  • Ground height above sea level, on a −5 to 25 m scale. Where a bare-earth reading exists (US only) it is shown as a point, with an outline around the spread of the rooftop-reading datasets. Where only surface models answered, you get the range they span instead of a point, and the coloured track goes grey: those models measure roofs and trees, so the colour would be a claim about safety that they have not earned.
  • Disagreement between datasets, out of 10 m, with a tick at the 25 cm of rise due by 2050. When the tick is buried at the far left of a red bar, you are looking at the whole point of this site.
  • Depth of the 1% flood over the ground, out of 5 m: FEMA's base flood elevation minus measured bare earth. It only exists where FEMA publishes a single base flood elevation and a bare-earth reading answered, so most of the time this card is empty and says the instruments can't see this here. That empty card is the product working, not failing. Older FEMA panels can use a different vertical datum (NGVD29, up to about 1.5 m off), which is why it carries a “check datum” label.

Every card also carries a confidence chip: regulatory map or bare earth when a real instrument can see the thing, amber when it can only bound it, and a hatched, empty meter when it cannot see it at all. A question mark means we asked and got no answer; a dash means there was nothing here to ask.

07

Why the flood map changes as you zoom

FEMA's server publishes a scale limit on its flood-zone layer: it will not draw zones any wider than 1:36,112, which is about zoom 14 on this map. Ask for more and it quietly returns a blank image. That is why the flood layer used to appear only when you were almost all the way in. The layer panel on the map now tells you which of three things you are looking at:

  • Native scale (zoom 14 and closer): FEMA's zones exactly as FEMA draws them.
  • Real zones, drawn smaller (zoom 10 to 14): FEMA's server decides scale from a tile's size in inches, so each tile is requested at a lower resolution setting, which makes the server draw it as if you were zoomed in. It is the same data, shrunk to fit. Measured on 23 September 2026 this costs 0.2–1.2 seconds a tile.
  • Overview, generalized (wider than zoom 10): the same trick at zoom 9 took up to 11 seconds a tile and at zoom 8 up to 26, far too slow to ask live. So the zones for the lower 48 were fetched once, ahead of time, at zoom 10, four states at a time, skipping every place FEMA's availability layer says has no digital map. Each fetched pixel was sorted into FEMA's classes by its colour. Then, going out one zoom at a time, every pixel of the overview records how much of its ground lies in each class: an exact area average, never “any zone anywhere in this pixel, so paint it.” On the map the colour is the class covering the most ground and the strength of the colour is how much ground that is, so a solid patch means most of the land there is mapped floodplain and a faint wash means some of it is. Zone D (FEMA has not studied the hazard) covers whole rural counties and is only ever drawn faintly, so it cannot read as the biggest danger on the continent.
    The colours keep FEMA's own families (blue for the 1% zone, orange for 0.2%, a hatched amber for “lower risk only because of a levee”) so they mean the same thing at every zoom, but they are stronger than FEMA's, because FEMA's pale wash disappears on a globe seen from space. The date the overview was fetched, and any states not yet fetched, are printed on the map's layer card. Alaska, Hawaii and the territories are not in the overview yet; there the map still shows FEMA's availability layer, where a digital flood map exists at all. From zoom 10 the live FEMA tiles take over.

The overview is a picture of FEMA's map, not a new analysis. It is exactly as current as the day it was fetched, it inherits every gap and every levee assumption in FEMA's map, and it answers nothing about a single address. For that, zoom in or drop a pin.

Outside the United States the panel says so plainly. There is no free global equivalent to draw, and the map opens on the whole planet so that this is visible before you read anything.

09

What happens to your address

There is no account, no sign-up, no email box, no analytics and no cookies on this site.

  • When you search a place name, that text goes to Nominatim, OpenStreetMap's public geocoder, and to nowhere else. It returns coordinates.
  • Coordinates — not your address — are then sent to the elevation and flood-map services listed above.
  • Your address is never written into the page URL, so it cannot end up in a browser history, a bookmark, a shared link, or a referrer header.
  • “Use my location” uses your browser's own geolocation. The coordinates are used in the page and sent to the same elevation services. They are not stored.
  • Results are cached in memory for the life of the tab and discarded when you close it.

Those third-party services have their own logs and their own policies; we do not control them, and pretending otherwise would be the sort of claim this whole site exists to avoid. If you would rather not send a place name anywhere at all, click the map instead — that path never calls a geocoder.

10

Who to actually ask

If you are deciding where to live, whether to buy, or whether your family is safe, this site is a starting point and not an answer. The people who genuinely know:

  • United States — the FEMA Map Service Center for the official panel, and your county floodplain manager, who will know what the map is missing. NOAA's Sea Level Rise Viewer for surveyed coastal inundation.
  • England — the Environment Agency's flood risk service.
  • Netherlands — LIWO and the national risk viewers.
  • Elsewhere — your national mapping agency or civil protection authority. Many publish far better data than anything available globally.
  • Anywhere — an elevation certificate from a licensed surveyor is the only thing that actually measures the ground at your house. It costs a few hundred dollars and it is the real version of what this site is estimating badly.

If Surge has been useful, it has probably been by telling you that it could not help, early enough for you to go and ask someone who can.
